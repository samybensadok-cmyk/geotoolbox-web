SEO for healthcare still comes down to being the provider Google shows when someone nearby searches for care. What changed is the first step. Some patients now ask an AI assistant to recommend a doctor. In our test, those shortlists drew on provider websites, Business Profiles and directories.

This guide is for practices, multi-specialty groups, health systems and telehealth providers, and their marketers.

## What SEO for Healthcare Means in 2026

**SEO for healthcare** (search engine optimization for medical providers, also called healthcare SEO or medical SEO) is the work of getting a practice, clinic, hospital or telehealth service found by people looking for care. That now happens in Google's map results, in the organic results below them, and in the AI answers some patients read before they click anything.

The mechanics match local and content SEO elsewhere. Three things make healthcare different.

**The trust bar is higher.** Google says E-E-A-T (experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trust) "isn't a specific ranking factor", but its systems [give even more weight to content with strong E-E-A-T](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) on topics that can affect someone's health. Google calls these "Your Money or Your Life" (YMYL) topics, and most pages a provider publishes fall into that group.

**Compliance shapes the SEO work itself.** Tracking tags, appointment forms, review replies and patient stories all touch HIPAA or FTC rules. A healthcare marketing team cannot run them the way an ecommerce team would.

**The searcher is often looking for a person.** A search like "cardiologist near me" is answered with doctors, offices and profiles, not articles. That puts much of the work on provider and location pages.

## Start with Your Organization Type

The right first move depends on what kind of organization you run. Many healthcare SEO guides are written for a single practice. A health system with dozens of locations has a different problem: too many pages, owned by too many teams.

Treat the table as a starting order. Telehealth-only providers usually cannot have a Business Profile at all: Google's guidelines require a location customers can visit or a business that travels to them. They compete on condition pages, state-availability pages and directories instead.

Health systems should also give each condition an owner and consolidate pages that duplicate each other, or hospitals and departments end up competing in search.

## What AI Answers Cite When Patients Ask for a Doctor

To see where AI answers about doctors come from, we ran our own test.

### How We Tested

In one run on November 15, 2026, we asked ChatGPT (with web search on), Gemini, Google AI Mode and Perplexity 8 patient questions from a US location. They covered specialists, a heart surgery hospital, urgent care, a pediatrician, fertility, primary care and telehealth therapy that takes insurance, across seven US cities. Each prompt ended with "Cite sources."

We counted each cited source once per answer: for ChatGPT only pages it also retrieved, and for Perplexity only inline citations. That left 174 sources across 32 answers. Perplexity answered through its Sonar API, and ChatGPT and Gemini each answered 2 of the 8 prompts through their API instead of the consumer app.

### What the Engines Cited

Directory and ranking pages were the only type of source all four engines cited, but the mix around them differed by engine.

Hospital and practice sites supplied **26 of ChatGPT's 43** sources and **46 of Gemini's 67**. Business Profile cards were **18 of AI Mode's 24**. Directories and ranking lists, from listing sites like Solv, WebMD and Zocdoc to editorial best-of articles, were **29 of Perplexity's 40**.

Every answer named specific providers, and none refused. 19 of the 32 added a caveat, such as checking insurance or availability: every ChatGPT and Perplexity answer, but only 2 from Gemini (both API answers) and 1 from AI Mode.

### What It Means for Providers

In this test, provider websites made up most of ChatGPT's and Gemini's counted citations, and 7 of AI Mode's 18 Business Profile cards were individual doctors' profiles. Directory listings and ranking lists showed up everywhere. That suggests generative engine optimization (GEO) for healthcare starts with the same work as local SEO: accurate listings, a presence on ranking lists, and detailed provider and location pages. ## Local SEO for Healthcare: Profiles, Reviews and Directories

When a patient searches for a doctor or clinic nearby, Google often shows Google Business Profile listings in a map before the organic results. For a practice, the profile often matters more than any page on the website. Our guide to [local SEO](https://geotoolbox.ai/blog/local-seo) covers the general mechanics. Healthcare adds three wrinkles.

### Practitioner and Location Profiles

Google treats doctors as individual practitioners. Its [Business Profile guidelines](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177?hl=en) say a public-facing practitioner who can be reached at the verified location may have their own profile, while support staff should not. A doctor should not have a separate profile for each specialty.

When several doctors work at one location, the practice gets its own profile for that location, and each doctor's profile carries only their name, not the practice name. A solo doctor at a branded group's location should share one profile with the group, named "Brand: Doctor Name". Hospital departments can have their own profiles when they operate as distinct public-facing entities, and each must use a category different from the main hospital's.

Get this wrong and you split reviews across duplicates, or invite suspensions. Map every provider and location before anyone creates a new listing.

### Reviews Without Breaking the Rules

Ask every patient for a review after the visit, through a review or texting vendor that signs a business associate agreement (BAA), and never only the ones you expect to be happy. Google's [contribution policy](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114?hl=en) bars offering any incentive, soliciting only positive reviews, pressuring people on the premises and requesting specific content. Behavioral health providers should check their professional ethics code before asking current clients.

The FTC's [final rule on fake reviews](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/08/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-testimonials), announced August 14, 2024, bans fake reviews and buying reviews a business knew or should have known were fake, incentives conditioned on a positive or negative review, undisclosed reviews from officers and managers, and unfounded threats used to suppress negative ones. Replying to reviews carries its own HIPAA risk, covered below.

### Directories Worth Your Time

Directory pages appeared in AI answers in the test above, with Zocdoc cited most often, and they also rank in search. Search your main services plus your city, note which directories show up on the first pages of results, and claim and correct those profiles first. Keep each practice's, doctor's and location's name, address and phone number (NAP) consistent across its own listings. Judge paid marketplaces on booked patients, not on listing views.

Links and mentions come from the same local world: hospital affiliations, medical society listings, local press and community sponsorships.

## Provider, Location and Service-Line Pages

Healthcare sites rank on provider, location and service (or condition) pages. Every provider, location and service page should link to both of the other types, so a patient can move from a symptom to a doctor to a nearby office in a couple of clicks.

**Service and condition pages** do the ranking work for "knee replacement" or "migraine treatment" searches. Pick which ones get pages from Search Console queries and keyword research, not from the org chart.

Write them around what patients ask before they book: symptoms in plain words, which insurance plans you accept, a realistic price range where you can give one, how long recovery takes, and a booking link. Many patients search a symptom rather than the clinical name of the service, so use both.

Open each page with a short, direct answer to the main question. Then break up the detail into paragraphs and sections with headings, as Google's [SEO starter guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/seo-starter-guide) advises.

**Provider pages** answer "who will I see?" List credentials, specialties, the locations where the doctor practices, languages, and whether they take new patients. Generate them from one provider database, so a doctor who leaves disappears from every page at once; redirect or retire the old profile URL separately.

**Location pages** need one page per real office, each with its own address, hours, services and providers. Add parking details and an embedded map too, because patients use these pages to plan the visit, and link each location's Business Profile to its own page, not the homepage. Pages for towns where you have no office, with the city name swapped in, are what Google's [spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) describe as doorway abuse: "multiple domain names or pages targeted at specific regions or cities that funnel users to one page."

Skip provider-by-location-by-service pages unless patients search for those combinations.

## YMYL Content and Medical Review

Google's own guidance puts trust first: of the four parts of E-E-A-T, it says [trust is most important](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content).

That guidance does not require a doctor to write or review every page, and a "medically reviewed by" line is not a ranking switch on its own. A named reviewer makes the page's claims accountable to patients and to Google's quality raters.

A workable standard for clinical pages:

1. A clinician on staff reviews the page before it goes live and again on a set schedule
2. The page shows the reviewer's name, credentials and the review date
3. Any statistic or treatment claim links to its source
4. The markup matches what readers see: schema.org's [MedicalWebPage](https://schema.org/MedicalWebPage) type has `reviewedBy` and `lastReviewed` properties for this

Then choose topics you can win. A regional clinic is unlikely to outrank national health publishers on "symptoms of high blood pressure". It can rank for the conditions it treats, in the places it treats them, with its own clinicians' answers.

AI drafting is fine for outlines and first drafts if a clinician edits every page. Google's [spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) list "using generative AI tools or other similar tools to generate many pages without adding value for users" as scaled content abuse. We go deeper on what makes content citable in [E-E-A-T for AI search](https://geotoolbox.ai/blog/eeat-ai-search).

## HIPAA, Tracking and Review Replies

HIPAA decides which analytics you can run and how you can answer reviews. This is a summary of the published guidance, not legal advice. Check your setup with your privacy officer or counsel.

### Tracking Pixels and Analytics

The HHS Office for Civil Rights (OCR) [guidance on online tracking technologies](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/hipaa-online-tracking/index.html) says tracking tags on pages behind a login, such as a patient portal or telehealth platform, "generally have access to PHI", meaning protected health information. It also flags public pages that let someone book an appointment or use a symptom checker without logging in.

One part of that guidance is gone. On June 20, 2024, a federal court in Texas (American Hospital Association v. Becerra) [vacated the part](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/hipaa-online-tracking/index.html) saying an IP address plus a visit to a public page about a health condition or provider, by itself, triggers HIPAA. HHS left the rest of the bulletin posted and says it is "evaluating its next steps."

That ruling does not make ad pixels on public pages risk-free. Where HIPAA does not apply, as in the bulletin's example of a mobile health app, it points to the FTC Act and the FTC's Health Breach Notification Rule. The FTC [ordered BetterHelp to pay $7.8 million](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/07/ftc-gives-final-approval-order-banning-betterhelp-sharing-sensitive-health-data-advertising) in 2023 over health data allegedly shared with Facebook and others for advertising.

Google draws its own line. Its [HIPAA page for Google Analytics](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/13297105?hl=en) says Google "does not offer Business Associate Agreements" for Analytics, that tags should stay off authenticated pages, and that public pages related to providing care are "more likely to be HIPAA-covered."

Before any tag goes live, run each page type through the same questions: is it a portal, login or registration page, does it collect health details, is it a public information page, and is the disclosure permitted under a BAA?

### Replying to Reviews

A reply that confirms the reviewer is your patient, or mentions their care, can be a disclosure of PHI. In 2022, OCR settled with New Vision Dental for [$23,000](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/compliance-enforcement/agreements/new-vision/index.html) after the practice disclosed patient information in replies to online reviews. Keep replies general, thank the reviewer for the feedback, and invite them to call the office.

Patient stories and testimonials need the patient's written HIPAA authorization first. OCR's 2016 [$25,000 settlement](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/compliance-enforcement/agreements/complete-pt/index.html) with Complete P.T. followed testimonials posted with patients' names and photos but no valid authorization.

## Technical SEO and Medical Schema

A common technical problem on large healthcare sites is the find-a-doctor directory. Some are search widgets that load provider results with JavaScript after the page opens. If every provider page is reachable only through that search box, crawlers may never find them, and AI crawlers that do not run scripts see no provider list at all. Give every provider and location a plain HTML link from a crawlable index page, and list those pages in an [XML sitemap](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/sitemaps/overview). Our [JavaScript SEO guide](https://geotoolbox.ai/blog/javascript-seo) shows how to check what bots receive.

Then make sure crawlers are allowed in. Google's AI features guidance says to allow crawling "in robots.txt, and by any CDN or hosting infrastructure", and ChatGPT and Perplexity search with their own bots, listed in our guide to [AI crawlers](https://geotoolbox.ai/blog/ai-crawlers).

The rest is standard technical SEO: fast mobile pages that pass Core Web Vitals, clean redirects after any site rebuild, and no pop-ups covering the content patients came for. Add accessibility to the same checklist: heading order, alt text, color contrast, keyboard navigation and form labels, tested against [WCAG](https://www.w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/wcag/), the W3C accessibility standard.

## Measure Appointments, Not Rankings

Track booked appointments, calls and completed forms, split by service line and location, in your scheduling system or a CRM covered by a BAA.

A realistic first six months puts measurement and compliance fixes first, then the pages, then links and pruning.

Three checks to run:

1. **Search Console** shows which queries and pages bring visits
2. **Call and form handling** catches the leak after the click: missed calls and slow callbacks waste rankings you already earned. Use call-tracking, form, scheduling and review-request vendors that sign a BAA, and keep appointment details out of analytics and ad platforms
3. **AI answers** for your core patient questions, checked monthly, show whether engines name you or a competitor

If you work with an agency, ask how they will measure success and in what timeframe. Google's own advice on [hiring an SEO](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/do-i-need-seo) adds a simple filter: if they guarantee you first place in search results, find someone else.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long does SEO take for a medical practice?

It depends on how competitive your specialty is in your city and how much of the site needs fixing first. Profile and page fixes come before content and links, so the earliest changes often show up in local results.

### How much does healthcare SEO cost?

We found no neutral price benchmark. Quotes depend on the number of locations, providers and pages, and on whether content, technical and local work are included. Compare proposals on scope and on how they will report booked appointments, not on ranking promises.

### Is SEO being replaced by AI search?

No. AI answers changed where patients see results, not what those results are built from. In our test, the engines cited provider websites, Business Profiles, directories and ranking lists, which are the same assets healthcare SEO builds.
