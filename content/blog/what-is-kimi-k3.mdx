At launch, asking an AI assistant what Kimi K3 was showed the problem in real time. With web search switched off, Gemini answered "I do not have reliable, verified information about a model called Kimi K3," and Claude said much the same. The model launched on July 16, 2026. The assistants most people rely on had not caught up, and that lag lasts months for any new model.

So here is the plain version, current as of October 26, 2026: what Kimi K3 actually is, what K3.1 Pro changes, whether the launch-day hype survives contact with the numbers, what it costs, whether you can run it, and how it stacks up against Kimi K2 and DeepSeek. We will flag which claims are Moonshot's own and which have been checked by someone independent, because on a launch-week model that distinction is most of the story.

## What Is Kimi K3?

**Kimi K3 is Moonshot AI's 2.8-trillion-parameter open-weight [mixture-of-experts](https://geotoolbox.ai/blog/what-is-deepseek) model, launched on July 16, 2026, with a one-million-token context window. It is the largest open-weight model any lab has released, and the weights went public on July 27 under Moonshot's own Kimi K3 License.** Only a small fraction of those parameters fire on any given token, which is the design trick that makes a model this size runnable at all.

The more useful thing to understand is what Kimi K3 is not. It is available in Kimi's own apps and API, but it does not beat everything else, and it is not the budget option Moonshot built its name on. It is a specialist: strong on coding, agent workflows, and long-context tasks, priced and positioned as a frontier model, and noticeably weaker outside that lane. One developer testing it on launch day put it bluntly: in his initial testing it was "materially worse than GPT-5.6 Sol and Fable 5 for certain use cases that do not involve coding."

Kimi K3 is the base of the newest tier in the same lineup we cover in our [Kimi AI explainer](https://geotoolbox.ai/blog/what-is-kimi-ai), which walks through the K2 family, the open-weights model, and the safety and China questions in more depth. This article is about K3 specifically: the July release that pushed Moonshot from "the cheap Chinese open model" into direct frontier competition, and what that shift means once you look past the headline benchmarks.

## Who Makes Kimi K3? Moonshot AI and the "DeepSeek Moment"

Kimi K3 comes from Moonshot AI, a Beijing lab founded in 2023 by Yang Zhilin and backed by Alibaba, Tencent, and Meituan. The company has climbed fast: valued at about $4.3 billion after its 2025 Series C, it raised about $2 billion in May 2026 at a valuation of about $20 billion. Bloomberg-sourced reports in late July had it closing a $3.5 billion round at $35 billion, and in early October Bloomberg reported a further round closed at about $50 billion ahead of a planned Hong Kong IPO. That is a steep curve for a lab whose reputation was built almost entirely on giving its models away.

The launch landed as a geopolitical event, not just a product release. Reuters framed K3 as the world's largest open-weight AI system and reported that it arrived a month after the U.S. government abruptly withdrew Anthropic's Fable and Mythos models over security concerns, and that shares in Chinese rivals Zhipu and MiniMax fell sharply on the news, down 27.7% and 16.5% in Hong Kong. Until K3, Meituan's LongCat-2.0 and DeepSeek's V4-Pro had led China's AI industry at around 1.6 trillion parameters. Alibaba answered within days by previewing [Qwen3.8-Max](https://geotoolbox.ai/blog/qwen3-8-max), a claimed 2.4-trillion-parameter flagship whose text weights it later opened, on August 12, 2026, under a bespoke license.

The reaction split along a familiar line. The loud version, common on launch day, was that the gap between Chinese and U.S. labs has all but closed. The sober version, which we find more defensible, is that K3 narrows it to under three months on the tasks it is strongest at. Either way, neither settles whether the benchmark wins actually hold up.

## Kimi K3 Specs and Architecture

Kimi K3 holds 2.8 trillion parameters in total but activates only 16 of 896 experts for any given token, so the compute cost per token stays far below what the full size suggests. That sparse routing runs on a framework Moonshot calls Stable LatentMoE, which lets the model push that aggressive ratio without the training instability that usually comes with it. Moonshot's model card lists 104 billion active parameters. This routing of tokens through a handful of specialist sub-networks is the same mixture-of-experts pattern we break down in [how ChatGPT works](https://geotoolbox.ai/blog/how-does-chatgpt-work).

The headline architecture change is Kimi Delta Attention, a hybrid linear-attention design that, in Moonshot's own Kimi Linear research, decodes up to 6.3 times faster at long context. It is paired with Attention Residuals, which pull information across model depth for a claimed efficiency gain at minimal extra cost, and Gated MLA for sharper attention. Roughly three out of every four attention layers use the cheaper linear form, which is what cuts the memory footprint enough to make a 1M window practical rather than theoretical.

The practical specs matter more than the internals for most readers. Kimi K3 takes text, images, and video as input and returns text, with a [context window](https://geotoolbox.ai/blog/how-does-ai-search-work) of 1,048,576 tokens and a default output cap of 131,072 tokens, raisable to 1,048,576. Reasoning is always on, and the `reasoning_effort` control now accepts three levels, `low`, `high`, and `max`, defaulting to `max`; the sampling parameters are locked server-side. At launch only `max` was available, so early write-ups describe a single-setting model. Put simply: K3 always thinks, and unless you dial the effort down it thinks hard on every request, which shows up in both the speed and the bill.

The results worth leaning on today are the independently checked ones. At launch, [Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/kimi-k3) scored K3's overall Intelligence Index at 57. The index has since been re-versioned several times, re-weightings of the benchmark suite rather than changes to the model: the August version (v4.1.1) put K3 at 60, tied with GLM-5.3, and the September 4 version (v4.2) at 50. On v4.3.2, K3 scored 44 (43.6). That was fourth among open-weight models when we checked on October 18; by October 26 it was fifth, behind Xiaomi's MiMo-V2.6-Pro (46), DeepSeek's V4.1 Flash (45.1), GLM-5.3 (45) and StepFun's Step 5 Preview (43.7), and well behind closed models such as Claude Opus 5.5 (58), Sonnet 5.5 (56), Fable 5.1 (53) and GPT-6 Astra (53). Its long-horizon knowledge-work Elo of 1547 trailed only Fable 5 when measured at launch. The Frontend Code Arena, a human-preference leaderboard, ranked K3 first at launch, ahead of Fable 5 and GPT-5.6 Sol. On the board dated October 16, 2026 it was 14th at 1,670; on the October 22 board it is 17th at 1,669, behind 16 entries led by Claude Opus 5.5 (1,849), including Kimi K3.1 Pro, Gemini 4 Argon, Qwen3.8-Max and the MIT-licensed DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, so it is no longer the highest-ranked open-weight model Arena lists. Critics note that leaderboard leans heavily on frontend and design tasks, so read it as coding-flavor strength rather than general capability. Read together, those say something specific: K3 was at the frontier on narrow coding and agent tasks at launch, and has since slipped from the open-weight intelligence lead and the top of the Arena board.

Everything else in the headline tables is Moonshot's own reporting, and the caveats are real. The company ran different benchmarks through different agent harnesses (its own Kimi Code, Claude Code, or Codex) at maximum thinking effort, so the comparisons are not strictly apples to apples. There is also a result the vendor page does not headline: on Artificial Analysis's AA-Omniscience test, K3 answers 47.6% of questions correctly and has a 53.2% hallucination rate, against 66.2% and 58.6% for Claude Opus 5.5.

## Kimi K3 Pricing: "Open" Does Not Mean Cheap

The biggest surprise of the July launch was not a benchmark. It was the price. Kimi K3's API costs $3.00 per million input tokens, $0.30 per million on a cache hit, and $15.00 per million output tokens. Moonshot's official USD rate card confirms those figures and lists its October 23 successor, K3.1 Pro, at 33% less on all three (details below); we break down the full lineup, the tier system, and the access routes in our [Kimi API pricing](https://geotoolbox.ai/blog/kimi-api-pricing) guide. If you are weighing the consumer app rather than the API, the subscription tiers and the credit system are covered in our [Kimi pricing](https://geotoolbox.ai/blog/kimi-pricing) guide.

Against Moonshot's own history, the jump is stark. That output rate is nearly four times what the [K2.7 Code](https://geotoolbox.ai/blog/kimi-api-pricing) model charged, and the input price is more than three times higher. K3.1 Pro's $10 output is still 2.5 times K2.7 Code's $4. As The Decoder put it, Chinese providers "aren't offering their frontier models at rock-bottom prices anymore." The era of a Chinese open model automatically being the budget pick is over.

Whether $15 hurts depends entirely on your workload. Because K3 reasons on every request and can be verbose, a single task often drags a long thinking trace, retried tool calls, and a growing history through the output meter, so heavy output bills are routine rather than rare. On Artificial Analysis's cost-per-task measure K3 runs $2.00 per Intelligence Index task: about nine times Claude Haiku 5.5 ($0.21, with an index of 43.4) and almost three times GPT-6.1 Sol ($0.72). ### The 1M Context Comes With Conditions

The headline context window and the one you can actually reach can differ for two reasons, and neither is on the spec sheet.

The first is a payload ceiling in Kimi Code. Moonshot's Kimi Code docs (updated October 24) list a 2 MB cap on the total size of a request, with the error `total message size N exceeds limit 2097152`, as the most frequent 400 error. In July one user hit that error in Kimi Code at roughly 770,000 tokens of his session, well short of the advertised 1,048,576, and closed the issue as fixed on July 29. So in Kimi Code, a text-heavy conversation can hit the byte cap before the token limit.

The second is a paywall. On Kimi's plans, K3 and K3.1 Pro access and the 1M window depend on the tier, and the two products differ: in Kimi Code, the 1M-token K3 window starts at Pro; in chat, Kimi's pricing table lists 1M-token K3 conversations only on Max and Ultra. If you are subscribing for the million-token window, check which product you need first.

### The Subscription Burn Rate Is the Part That Surprised People

Per-token pricing is only half the story, and it is not the half that bit early users hardest. The consistent first-week complaint across Kimi's coding plans is that a single task can consume a startling share of a usage window. One user on the $19 plan ran a task he benchmarks every model against and watched it eat almost his entire five-hour allowance, where the same task on a $20 OpenAI plan finished in minutes and barely registered. Another on the same entry tier hit a loop retrying a Docker step and burned through a five-hour window, which was 20% of his weekly quota, on that one failure. A third, on a roughly $100 Kimi plan, reported quota draining at a pace similar to a $200 Anthropic subscription running Fable, and notably he liked the model, saying he "might put this above Opus 4.8."

The mechanism is measurable: Artificial Analysis's index run drew about 160 million output tokens from K3, above its 140 million median. In Simon Willison's test, a single SVG generation returned 16,658 output tokens of which 13,241 were reasoning, costing 25 cents for one image. One user described the trace pattern behind it: "paragraph after paragraph of 'Wait, actually...'" as the model second-guessed itself on one small detail.

This is where `reasoning_effort` stops being a footnote. At launch "max" was the only accepted value, with no cheaper mode to fall back to; Moonshot has since added `low` and `high`, but `max` is still the default, so every request you do not explicitly turn down pays full reasoning tokens at the $15 output rate. Max effort also makes K3 slow enough to break evaluation harnesses: one public comparison had to raise a five-minute per-task timeout to thirty, and one task still took around nine minutes. If cost or latency matters, set the effort level explicitly rather than leaving it at the default.

One more thing worth naming: almost nobody can measure their own burn. The plans do not say what a usage window means in tokens, so, as one user put it, limits are "hard to measure" and hard to compare across providers.

## What Kimi K3.1 Pro Changes

Moonshot released Kimi K3.1 Pro on October 23, 2026, calling it the first model in a new K3.1 series and "a post-trained version of Kimi K3, with substantially larger post-training compute." The architecture is not new: Artificial Analysis lists the same 2.8T total and 104B active parameters and a 1M-token context.

The prices are 33% lower than K3 on all three rates: $2.00 input, $10.00 output and $0.20 per million cached tokens.

The weights are not out yet. Moonshot says "the full model weights will be released by Nov 4," and when we checked on October 24 and again on October 26, Hugging Face, GitHub and ModelScope showed no K3.1 repository, so treat K3.1 Pro as an API model for now.

The benchmark split matters again. Moonshot reports 65.7% on Terminal-Bench 4.0, run on the Claude Code harness, against a published 66.4% for Claude Opus 5.5. Artificial Analysis, running its own tests, scores K3.1 Pro 55 on its Intelligence Index, 11 points above K3's 44 and fourth overall behind Opus 5.5 (58), Sonnet 5.5 (56) and Muse Wonder Full (56).

## Can You Actually Run Kimi K3?

"Open weights" sounds like you can download K3 and run it yourself. The weights are genuinely public now, released on July 27 to Moonshot's Hugging Face repo. Unsloth published GGUF quantizations right away, and mainline llama.cpp merged K3 support on August 15, but Ollama offers only a cloud-hosted tag. For almost everyone, though, downloadable is still not the same as runnable, and the reason is scale.

At 2.8 trillion parameters, the repository is about 1.56TB to download in K3's native 4-bit MXFP4 format, and full-speed inference needs at least that much fast accelerator memory for the weights alone. Moonshot recommends serving it on a supernode of 64 or more accelerators; the vLLM team puts the floor at one 8x B300 node or 16x B200 GPUs. A single RTX 4090 or a 512GB Mac Studio does not come close. One open-source project, deltafin, got it onto an M1 Max MacBook by streaming experts from disk and now reports about 3.4 seconds per token, which captures the gap between "I can technically load it" and "I can use it."

This is the tension practitioners keep circling: K3 may be legally open while staying operationally closed to anyone without a data center, unless they accept disk-backed speeds. What open weights buy you here is not laptop inference. A common framing in the community is that the real payoff is provider competition: because anyone can host K3, a market of API providers drives the price down, the way many independent hosts already do for models like GLM. Alongside that you get durability (a version cannot be silently retired out from under you) and data residency for teams that self-host. So who self-hosts? Organizations with real GPU infrastructure and a reason to keep data in-house, and even they mostly run it non-interactively. That is the practitioners' fix for the speed problem: point it at an overnight job like "analyze this codebase for vulnerabilities" rather than an interactive chat. A response an hour later is fine there. For everyone else, the practical paths are Moonshot's own platform, a router like OpenRouter, or the free tier in the Kimi consumer app. If you do want to try self-hosting, our [guide to running Kimi K3 locally](https://geotoolbox.ai/blog/how-to-run-kimi-k3-locally) walks through every route, the real hardware, and what each one costs. Note that API credits are billed separately and are not bundled into any Kimi app subscription, so paying for the app does not hand you API access. Our [open weights vs open source](https://geotoolbox.ai/blog/open-weights-vs-open-source) explainer covers why the distinction matters, and our [best open-source LLMs](https://geotoolbox.ai/blog/best-open-source-llms) ranking includes a self-host reality check for this class of model.

## Kimi K3 vs Kimi K2 vs DeepSeek: Which Should You Use?

The short answer: reach for Kimi K3 or K3.1 Pro only when the job specifically needs what they do best, and keep something cheaper or more reliable for everything else. K3 earns its 3-to-4x premium over K2 (K3.1 Pro narrows that to roughly 2-to-2.5x) when you need the long context (bearing in mind Kimi Code's 2 MB request cap and the plan tiering above), native vision, or frontier-grade coding and agent performance, and when you can tolerate its speed, which Artificial Analysis currently puts at about 52 output tokens per second for both K3.1 Pro and K3 (checked October 26), with a lot of thinking in between; Moonshot's K3.1 Pro Highspeed variant claims 2-3x that speed at double the price.

For most day-to-day work the math favors its own siblings. Kimi K2.6 and K2.7 Code are far cheaper, fully open, and already strong on coding, so unless a task hits K3's specific strengths, the older models do it for a fraction of the cost. Starting October 31, 2026, K2 series models will no longer be available to newly registered users, and Moonshot plans to retire them by the end of December 2026. If you are optimizing purely for price per token, [DeepSeek](https://geotoolbox.ai/blog/what-is-deepseek) V4 is cheaper still. And where the cost of a wrong answer is high, Claude Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.1 keep the edge on careful reasoning and verified coding. A fuller side-by-side of the open Chinese models lives in our [Chinese AI models comparison](https://geotoolbox.ai/blog/chinese-ai-models-compared).

## What Kimi K3 Means for Your AI Visibility

Come back to where this started. A day after launch, the assistants most people use could not describe Kimi K3 because their training predates it, and they will stay behind for months. That lag is not a Kimi quirk. It is how every model treats anything new, including your business.

If a brand-new, heavily covered AI model is invisible to deployed assistants, then a product launch, a rebrand, or a corrected fact about your company is invisible the same way until training and the live web catch up. That gap is exactly what [AI visibility](https://geotoolbox.ai/blog/what-is-ai-visibility) work addresses. And because K3's weights are public, the model gets fine-tuned and embedded into a long tail of downstream tools you will never see individually, each answering questions about your market from whatever it can find about you.

That makes two things the actual levers, and neither is the model. The first is reachability: every one of these systems and the crawlers feeding them has to be able to fetch your site, or you are absent from the live layer that updates faster than training does. The second is consistency, the [core of getting cited by AI](https://geotoolbox.ai/blog/what-is-geo): the businesses described correctly are the ones whose facts line up across the sources a model reads. In our experience at geotoolbox, the companies that surface well in AI answers are rarely the ones with the prettiest homepage; they are the ones a model can find, parse, and trust without tripping over contradictions.

You cannot control what Kimi K3 or the next open model learns about you. You can control whether it can reach you at all. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is Kimi K3 Chinese?

Yes. Kimi K3 is built by Moonshot AI, a Beijing lab founded in 2023 and backed by Alibaba, Tencent, and Meituan. It is a Chinese company, but where your data goes depends on the product: Moonshot's API is run by its Singapore entity on servers in Singapore, while kimi.com and the mobile apps store data in China. That jurisdiction question is one reason the open weights matter: teams that need to keep data in their own jurisdiction can self-host rather than send prompts to Moonshot's servers.

### Is Kimi K3 open source?

Not quite. K3 is open weights, not open source. Moonshot released the model files on July 27, 2026 under its own Kimi K3 License, which permits commercial use but is not an OSI-approved open-source license: model-as-a-service operators whose aggregate revenue, with their affiliates, exceeds $20 million over any 12 months need a separate agreement with Moonshot, and products with more than 100 million monthly users or $20 million in monthly revenue must display "Kimi K3"; internal use and use through Moonshot's official products or certified inference partners are exempt from both. The company also does not release the training data or full recipe, and the Kimi app and API stay closed. So you can run and fine-tune the model, but you cannot fully reproduce how it was made.

### Can I run Kimi K3 on my own computer?

Realistically, no. The weights are public now, but at 2.8 trillion parameters K3 is about 1.56TB to download and needs at least that much fast memory for full-speed inference, so Moonshot recommends a supernode of 64 or more accelerators. That is far beyond any consumer machine: a 512GB Mac Studio does not come close, and the best-known attempt to force it onto a MacBook by streaming experts from disk runs at about 3.4 seconds per token. Self-hosting is practical only for organizations with serious GPU infrastructure, usually run as overnight batch jobs rather than interactive chat. For everyone else, the hosted API or a provider like OpenRouter is the route. Our [how to run Kimi K3 locally](https://geotoolbox.ai/blog/how-to-run-kimi-k3-locally) guide covers the routes and hardware in full.

### Is Kimi K3 better than Claude or GPT?

On narrow coding and agent benchmarks, K3 competed at the top at launch, but on the Frontend Code Arena board dated October 16, 2026 it was 14th, and on the October 22 board it was 17th, behind the open-weight DeepSeek V4.1 Flash. On general intelligence, Artificial Analysis's v4.3.2 index put it fourth among open-weight models on October 18 and fifth on October 26, and well behind the closed leaders, and reviewers report it is weaker on non-coding work. Its successor K3.1 Pro scores 55 on the same Artificial Analysis index, fourth overall. It is a strong specialist, not a clear overall winner.
