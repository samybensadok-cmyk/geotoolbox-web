## Semrush vs Ahrefs: The Short Answer

If you want one platform for the whole job, pick **Semrush**. It spans SEO, paid search, content, local, and social, its keyword database is wider, and it is the stronger fit for agencies and anyone who wants to see more of a small site's progress. If your work is led by backlinks and organic research and you value a cleaner interface and more conservative numbers, pick **Ahrefs**.

A couple of things hold no matter which you choose. First, the tools will disagree, often dramatically, because they run different crawlers, keyword databases, and traffic models. Second, neither number is the truth. For your own Google traffic, [Google Search Console](https://search.google.com/search-console/about) is the closest thing to ground truth, and both tools are best treated as competitive-intelligence estimates, not measurement. The rest of this article shows you exactly how far off they are, using our own data.

One thing holds across all three: Semrush reports more keywords on every site, even on the two where it was restricted to a single US database. The size of the gap is where we are more cautious. In our sample it was widest on the smallest site (about 31 times) and narrower on the large one (about three times), which fits the idea that Semrush's database catalogs more of the long-tail and low-volume terms a young site picks up first. A keyword count is database coverage, not proof that any of those keywords rank or send traffic. Which raises the obvious question.

More keywords is not more traffic, and it is not more accuracy. To find out whether the extra keywords Semrush reports are real or just noise, we pulled the actual clicks Google Search Console recorded for each site over the same 30-day window. GSC counts the real Google organic clicks your site received, not visits from every channel, which makes it the best first-party benchmark for exactly the thing the tools are trying to estimate.

The overshoot is large, and note that these tool estimates are modeled monthly organic traffic while GSC is actual clicks, so if anything the mismatch understates how far off the raw numbers run. On the large site both tools ran three to nearly five times over Google's roughly 84,000 clicks: Ahrefs estimated 287,000 and Semrush 398,000. On the tiny site both overshot by six to ten times, because a new site's handful of real clicks is almost impossible to model. The mid-size store is the interesting one, where Ahrefs undershot (2,929 vs 3,558 real) while Semrush ran high at 6,261. Semrush read higher than Ahrefs on both larger sites; only on the tiny site was Semrush the lower, and closer, of the two.

None of that makes either tool bad. It makes their "organic traffic" a modeled estimate, not a click counter. It also matches the most-cited public test on the question: a 2022 study in which Ahrefs checked its own estimates against Google Search Console on [1,635 websites](https://ahrefs.com/blog/traffic-estimations-accuracy/) and found a median deviation of 49.52% for Ahrefs and 68.36% for Semrush. One caveat, since we are the Semrush affiliate: Ahrefs ran and published that study, so read it as an interested party's finding, not a neutral referee. Its direction still lines up with what we saw, Semrush estimates run higher and looser, and with what practitioners repeat constantly: the estimates are useful for spotting trends and comparing competitors, never for reporting your own traffic. For that, open Search Console.

The gap has a narrow practical use. Your own site's ratio of real Search Console clicks to a tool's estimate can roughly sanity-check that tool's number for a competitor, but only a competitor of similar size and market. Emphasis on rough: our own three sites show the error swinging from a slight undershoot to a ten-times overshoot, so a ratio borrowed from one site will not transfer cleanly to a site of a different size or age. Treat it as a gut check, not a correction factor.

## Keyword Research: Database Breadth vs Click Realism

This is where the two tools show their personalities. Semrush's Keyword Magic Tool is built for breadth. It returns huge lists grouped into topic clusters with search intent attached, plus a Personal Keyword Difficulty score tuned to your domain. If your job is to find every angle on a topic and map coverage, Semrush surfaces more to work with, which is the same database advantage that produced the keyword gap above.

Ahrefs Keywords Explorer is built for realism. Its signature metric is Traffic Potential, the total traffic the top-ranking page for a term actually gets from all its variations, not just the head keyword's volume. It also reports a Clicks metric, search volume minus the searches that never click because an answer sits in the SERP. On a query where an AI Overview or featured snippet eats most of the clicks, that distinction is the difference between chasing a keyword and skipping it.

The database-size debate is mostly noise. Comparison articles quote figures for Ahrefs anywhere from 7 billion to 28.7 billion keywords depending on who is writing, which tells you these numbers are unreliable marketing counts, not audited facts. Both tools index tens of billions of keywords; that is plenty. What actually differs is philosophy. Semrush tends to group keyword variants together, which inflates volumes but widens coverage. Ahrefs reports lower, more conservative per-keyword numbers. Both approaches are defensible, and the AI answers you see today increasingly repeat both sets of figures as if they were settled, which they are not.

Verdict: Semrush for breadth and content planning, Ahrefs for judging whether a keyword will actually send clicks.

## Backlinks: Where Ahrefs Still Earns Its Reputation

Ahrefs built its name on backlinks, and it is still the default for link work, but the "bigger index" story has flipped on paper. By each vendor's own self-reported (and take-with-salt) count, Semrush now advertises the larger raw index, around 43 trillion links against Ahrefs's roughly 35 trillion. In an independent test, Style Factory checked 10 major domains and found Semrush reported more referring domains on nine of them, including Amazon (4.3 million vs Ahrefs's 2.2 million).

Then look at our own smallest site and it flips the other way. For geotoolbox.ai, Ahrefs found 64 referring domains and Semrush found 27. Same site, and the tool with the "smaller" index found more than twice as many links. The counts disagree in both directions, and raw index size tells you very little.

What separates them is quality and history. Ahrefs refreshes its link index constantly and keeps years of historical data, and its crawler has long been the practitioner favorite for link audits, disavow work, and finding a competitor's best links. A huge index full of dead or spammy links is worse than a smaller, cleaner one. If backlinks are the core of your work, Ahrefs remains the safer default, and our [best link building platforms](https://geotoolbox.ai/blog/best-link-building-platforms) guide covers the outreach tools that sit on top of whichever index you use. If you want backlinks as one part of a wider suite, Semrush's data is now genuinely competitive on volume.

## AI Search Visibility: The 2026 Battleground

Both tools now track how you show up inside AI answers, and both gate the deeper version behind paid tiers or add-ons. Semrush has an AI Visibility Toolkit that monitors your brand across ChatGPT, Google AI Overviews and AI Mode, Gemini, and Perplexity, priced per domain. Ahrefs has Brand Radar, which covers a similar set of engines, with fuller prompt tracking on higher plans. On features they are close; the deeper AI reporting sits behind an upgrade on both.

Here is a gap our own data hints at, and it matters most if AI search is where your audience is. geotoolbox.ai earns about 152 Google clicks a month, the tiny number you saw earlier, mostly because it is new and still ranking on page three or four. Yet both tools flag it appearing in AI answers: Ahrefs counts 27 ChatGPT responses across 9 of its pages, Semrush 18 ChatGPT-cited pages. We would not oversell one young site's citations as proof of anything, but it is visibility a Google-clicks report never registers. For an AI-first site, the keyword count is mainly a progress signal; whether engines actually cite you is a separate question, and one you only see if you track [AI share of voice](https://geotoolbox.ai/blog/ai-share-of-voice) directly.

The search suites and a dedicated citation tracker are complementary, not either-or: one measures where you rank, the other whether engines cite you. Wide keyword coverage still helps here, because AI engines fan a single prompt into [dozens of implied sub-questions](https://geotoolbox.ai/blog/query-fan-out), so seeing more of them tells you which your content can even answer. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Which is better, Semrush or Ahrefs?

For most people buying one tool, Semrush is the more versatile pick because it covers SEO, paid search, content, and local in one platform and surfaces more keywords. Ahrefs is better if your work is led by backlinks and organic research and you prefer a cleaner interface with more conservative numbers. Neither reports your real traffic, so pair whichever you choose with Google Search Console.

### Can you trust Semrush's traffic numbers?

Trust them for relative comparison and trend direction, not as literal visits. In our test across three sites, Semrush's organic-traffic estimate ran higher than Ahrefs's on the two larger sites and overshot actual Google Search Console clicks; on the smallest site both overshot, with Ahrefs the higher of the two. Both tools model traffic from rankings and click curves; only Search Console counts your real clicks.

### Why do Semrush and Ahrefs show different keyword counts for the same site?

Because they crawl the web with different databases, update schedules, and definitions of a ranking keyword. Semrush's keyword database is wider, so it reports more terms, especially long-tail and non-US ones. In our data the gap ranged from about 3 times more on a large site to 31 times more on a small new site.

### Do you need both Semrush and Ahrefs?

Most people do not. The overlap is large, and one tool plus Google Search Console covers the core job. Teams that do serious link building sometimes keep Ahrefs for its backlink index alongside Semrush for everything else, but paying for two full subscriptions is a cost most solos should skip.

### Is there a free alternative to Semrush and Ahrefs?

Google Search Console and Google Keyword Planner are free and cover your own rankings and basic keyword volumes. Both tools also offer limited free tiers, and free graders exist for specific jobs. Our [best free SEO tools](https://geotoolbox.ai/blog/best-free-seo-tools) guide covers the ones worth starting with before you pay.

### Which is better for a new or small website?

Semrush, in most cases, because it catalogs more of the keywords a young site starts ranking for, so you see progress Ahrefs may not register yet, just treat that larger count as coverage, not traffic. If budget is the deciding factor, both paid suites cost about the same at entry, so lean on the free options (Search Console, Keyword Planner, and each tool's free tier) first. Either way, treat the traffic estimates as directional until Search Console has enough data.
